Is a poll lead big enough to call the race?

A short guide to the Election Poll Beta-Binomial Analyzer: what it computes, why, and how to read its graphs. Each idea is given twice, once in plain words and once in statistical terms.

Executive summary

The tool takes a two-candidate poll and asks whether Candidate A's lead is large enough to say, at a chosen confidence level, that A's true support is above 50%. It uses a Bayesian Beta-Binomial model: a flat starting belief is updated with the poll counts, a central credible interval is drawn, and the race is called only when the low end of that interval clears 50%. When it can't make the call, it reports the minimum number of votes A would have needed in a poll of the same size.

At 95% confidence, A needs 9 of 10 votes (90%) in a 10-person poll, 60 of 100 (60%) in a 100-person poll, and 531 of 1,000 (53.1%) in a 1,000-person poll. Each example pair in this guide sits exactly on that boundary, so a single vote flips the verdict. The lead A needs shrinks roughly as 98/√n percentage points above 50%, so making the poll ten times larger only cuts the required lead by about a factor of three.

Two cautions apply. The rule is stricter than "95% sure A is ahead"; it behaves like a one-sided 97.5% test. And it measures sampling error only, not the other ways real polls go wrong.

What the tool does

You enter a two-way poll result (votes for Candidate A, votes for Candidate B) and a confidence level. The tool answers two questions: can A be projected as the winner at that confidence level, and if not, how many votes would A have needed out of the same total?

It is written in Python (analyze_election_poll_beta.py) with a small desktop GUI on top (election_poll_gui.py), ported from the original MATLAB function analyzeElectionPoll_beta.m.

The statistics, step by step

PlainlyPrecisely

  1. A poll is a count

    Every person polled is like one flip of a lopsided coin. The coin lands on A with some unknown probability: A's true level of support in the whole electorate. The poll only shows us the flips, not the coin.

    With n = votesA + votesB respondents, votesA is modelled as Binomial(n, p), where p is A's population share. Undecided and third-party answers are outside the model.

  2. Start with no opinion

    Before looking at the poll, the tool treats every level of support, from 0% to 100%, as equally believable. It brings no hunch about who is ahead.

    The prior is the uniform, uninformative Beta(1, 1) distribution on p ∈ [0, 1].

  3. Let the poll reshape that belief

    The poll turns the flat starting belief into a hump centred near A's share of the vote. The more people polled, the narrower and taller the hump, because the evidence is stronger.

    Beta is conjugate to the Binomial, so the posterior is again a Beta, obtained by adding the counts:

    p | data ~ Beta(1 + votesA, 1 + votesB)

    Its mean is (votesA + 1)/(n + 2) and its variance shrinks roughly as 1/n. This is the blue curve in the graphs.

  4. Mark out the plausible range

    Trim away the least believable 2.5% at each end of the hump. What remains is the range where A's true support lies with 95% probability. This is the shaded band in the graphs.

    A central credible interval, with α = 1 − confLevel:

    [ F⁻¹(α/2), F⁻¹(1 − α/2) ]

    F⁻¹ is the inverse CDF of the posterior Beta: scipy.stats.beta.ppf in Python, betainv in MATLAB. It is a direct probability statement about p, given the data and the prior.

  5. Make the call

    If even the pessimistic end of that range sits above 50%, A is projected to win. If the range still reaches below 50%, the poll can't rule out a loss, so no call is made.

    Decision rule:

    isWinner ⇔ F⁻¹(α/2) > 0.5

    The black vertical line in the graphs is the 0.5 threshold.

  6. Work out what it would take

    When no call is made, the tool keeps the poll size fixed and asks: what if A had one more vote? Two more? It stops at the first count where the pessimistic end clears 50%. That is the red dashed curve.

    With k scanned from votesA to n:

    reqVotes = min { k : F⁻¹Beta(1+k, 1+n−k)(α/2) > 0.5 }

    If no k ≤ n qualifies, the sample is too small for that confidence level and the tool returns None / NaN.

The GUI

  • votesA, votesB: the poll counts, as whole numbers. Zero is allowed for either one, but not both.
  • confLevel: pick a preset or type your own value between 0 and 1. 0.9545 and 0.9973 match the familiar 2σ and 3σ levels of a normal distribution.
  • Show Results (or press Enter): writes the verdict to the log and opens a new graph window.
  • Close All Figures: closes every graph window and leaves the GUI open.

Eight out of ten sounds like a landslide, but ten people tell you very little. A's true support could plausibly be anywhere from about 48% to 94%, so even an 80% showing can't rule out a loss. It takes 9 out of 10 before the call is made.

How poll size n changes everything

The three example pairs show the same rule at three scales. What changes is how wide the plausible range is, and so how big a lead A needs before the whole range clears 50%.

10,0005,09950.99%0.99 pts50.98%0.020

Bigger polls let smaller leads count, but with diminishing returns. Going from 100 to 1,000 people cuts the lead A needs from 10 points to about 3. Going on to 10,000 people cuts it only to about 1 point. To halve the lead you need, you have to poll four times as many people.

Very small polls are a different world. With 10 people, only a near-sweep counts, and the familiar rule of thumb underestimates how much is needed.

Near p = 0.5 the posterior sd is about √(p(1−p)/n) ≈ 0.5/√n. The call is made when mean − 1.96 sd > 0.5, which gives

share needed ≈ 0.5 + 0.98 / √n

The lead therefore scales as n−1/2. For small n the normal approximation breaks down: the Beta posterior is skewed and vote counts are discrete. That is why the exact curve sits above the approximation and zigzags below about n = 50, with 90% needed at n = 10 against 81% predicted.

Subtleties and limits

The rule is stricter than "95% sure A is ahead"

Look at the "chance A's true support > 50%" rows. For 8 vs 2, 59 vs 41 and 530 vs 470 it is 96.7%, 96.4% and 97.1%. All three are above 95%, yet none of these polls is called. The reason is that the tool checks the low end of a two-sided 95% interval. That leaves only 2.5% in the lower tail, so it is effectively a one-sided test at 97.5%.

If "95% sure A is ahead" is the question you want answered, use the one-sided rule, P(p > 0.5) ≥ 0.95. Under that rule all three would be called, and the thresholds drop to 8 of 10, 59 of 100 and 527 of 1,000.

What the model leaves out

The tool measures sampling error only. It assumes a simple random sample from the electorate and exactly two choices. It does not account for undecided voters, turnout, non-response, survey weighting, or house effects between pollsters. Real polls carry these extra uncertainties, so treat "projected to win" as a statement about this sample, not a forecast of the election.

Take-home

  • A lead only means something relative to poll size. 60% settles it at n = 100, is nowhere near enough at n = 10, and is a large margin at n = 1,000.
  • The lead needed shrinks with the square root of n. At 95% it is about 98/√n points above 50%. Quadrupling the poll halves the lead required.
  • Small polls need near-sweeps. Below about 50 respondents, the exact Beta answer is noticeably stricter than the rule of thumb.
  • Borderline verdicts are borderline. In every example pair, one vote flips the call. Read a result near the threshold as "too close to call", whichever side it lands on.
  • Know which question the rule answers. The two-sided 95% rule acts like 97.5% one-sided certainty. Switch to P(p > 0.5) ≥ 0.95 if that is the question you mean.
  • Sampling error is only part of the story. Real-world polling error usually adds to it.
