1Summary

Across both mailboxes there are 22 on-site visits and 11 offers. Seven offers followed a visit; four came without one. Put together, they make 26 entries — the complete record of every time an employer met Chang in person or said yes in writing.

  • Four offers with no on-site. Apple (February 2013, via Red Oak) hired him on a recruiter’s negotiation alone; Wipro (June–July 2013) made its offer after phone-bridge rounds; ELORET (November 2014) sent a draft and a formal letter with no visit on record; and BP (mid-2017) made a verbal offer for a Research and Chemometrics Scientist role in Naperville, Illinois, after a phone and video-panel process — which Chang turned down over pay. Apple is the only one of the four that became a job.
  • Ten of eleven offers fell inside 23 months. Nine date from 2013 and one from November 2014 (ELORET); the eleventh, BP in mid-2017, is the only offer of the whole JDSU/Viavi decade after ELORET.
  • On-sites converted only while the search was hot. Of the 15 visits up to July 2013, seven produced offers. Of the seven visits from December 2013 onwards, none did.
  • Place decided the outcome. Every offer outside California — Goodyear (Ohio), Intel (Oregon), 1st Detect (Texas) and BP (Illinois) — was turned down. All three offers that became jobs were in the Bay Area: BD Biosciences in San Jose, Apple in Sunnyvale and JDSU in Santa Rosa.
  • A city for 24 of the 26 entries. Six come straight from the emails, one (BP) from Chang himself, the rest from public records of the company’s site at the time or from clues in the emails. KPMG’s office and the Wipro role’s location are not recorded.

2.1Two mailboxes, one list

Note — this report combines Gmail and Yahoo. The 21 on-site visits verified in the Gmail report (its §5.1) are all here. One more is added: 1st Detect, June 2013. The BP process is in Gmail (thread #173, 24 emails, June–September 2017), but the archive kept only its first eight messages, all from June 26–28 — so the interview itself, the verbal offer and the decline fall in the part that was never captured. 7Discussion

7.1The conversion window

Up to July 2013, seven of fifteen on-site visits ended in offers — nearly one in two, far above typical final-round conversion. From December 2013 onwards, none of seven did. The break coincides with joining JDSU in early September 2013: every later visit was made by an employed candidate weighing a move against a good job, and several were followed by his own withdrawals or postponements (recorded in the Gmail report). ELORET (2014) and BP (2017) show the market had not closed — offers still came — but both came without a visit, and no later on-site produced one.

7.3Why four offers needed no visit

Three of the four offers without an on-site share a route: an intermediary or an existing relationship carried the trust that a visit usually supplies. Apple and Wipro both came through staffing vendors (Red Oak and ReqRoute) and were effectively contract-placement decisions, where a résumé and phone rounds were enough. ELORET worked through a single named contact who sent a draft letter before the formal one — a direct approach rather than an open hiring round. BP is the exception: a large company’s own recruiter, a full-time scientist role, and a formal panel — held by video instead of on site. Of the four, it is the one that shows where hiring was heading: by 2017 a thorough remote panel could replace the visit entirely.

7.4Limitations

8Take-home message

Twenty-two visits and eleven offers — and the three he took were the three that kept him in the Bay Area.

Read as one list, the in-person record has a simple shape. It is concentrated in eighteen months from mid-2012 to the end of 2013, when on-sites converted at close to one in two; after Chang joined JDSU, visits continued for five more years but no longer led to offers. Four offers needed no visit at all — three carried by an intermediary or a personal contact, and BP’s by a remote panel in 2017 — and one of them, Apple, briefly became his job. Across all eleven offers, the deciding factor was not salary or title but place: everything outside California was declined, and every job he took was in the Bay Area.
