Point Estimates in the CasinoMLE & MAP, Step by Step

From the MATLAB script multi_armed_bandit_demo_MLE_MAP_TS.m

A gambler faces five slot machines with unknown win rates and a budget of 2,000 pulls. Two of the three strategies in the script — Maximum Likelihood (MLE) and Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) — are point-estimate methods: both reduce each arm to a single number, then greedily pick the largest. This note walks through what those two estimators actually compute, turn by turn, and why MAP is essentially MLE with a layer of "fake data" glued on top.

I. The Shared Scaffolding

Both algorithms share the same bookkeeping. On every pull, regardless of strategy, three counters for the chosen arm are updated:

N(arm)++  ·  S(arm) += reward  ·  F(arm) += (1 − reward)

The script also hard-codes a round-robin kickoff for the first K pulls: at t = 1 arm 1 is pulled, at t = 2 arm 2, and so on through t = 5. This serves two purposes — it guarantees every arm has at least one data point (so S/N is defined for everyone), and it puts all three algorithms on identical footing when real decision-making begins at t = 6.

II. MLE — The Greedy Empiricist

From t = 6 onward, MLE picks the arm with the highest observed success rate:

θ̂kMLE  =  SkNk   →   arm = argmaxk θ̂kMLE

No prior, no uncertainty, no memory of when successes happened — only the running totals matter. Ties (common early on when every arm has Nk = 1 and the means are just 0 or 1) are broken by a tiny random jitter of order 10⁻⁹, which amounts to a uniform random pick among tied arms.

Failure Mode After a lucky 2-for-2 start, an arm shows θ̂ = 1.0 and can crowd out every competitor forever. A single failure on a well-established leader barely dents its mean (18/25 → 18/26), so once an arm builds a lead, MLE locks onto it — even if it isn't actually the best. This is the mechanism Figure 7 of the script is designed to expose.

III. MAP — MLE with Pseudo-Counts

MAP treats each arm's unknown success rate as a random variable with a Beta(α₀, β₀) prior. After observing Sk successes and Fk failures, the posterior is Beta(α₀ + Sk, β₀ + Fk), and MAP reports its mode:

θ̂kMAP  =  α₀ + Sk − 1 α₀ + β₀ + Nk − 2

With the script's choice of α₀ = β₀ = 4, this simplifies to a form that makes the mechanism transparent:

θ̂kMAP  =  3 + Sk 6 + Nk

The 3 comes from α₀ − 1 = 4 − 1. The 6 comes from α₀ + β₀ − 2 = 4 + 4 − 2. Both subtractions are part of the standard Beta-mode formula; neither is arbitrary.

Where does the prior come from?

In this script: the programmer, on line 34. The Beta(4, 4) prior was chosen for pedagogical reasons — it is symmetric around 0.5, contributes a pseudo-count of 6 "imaginary observations," and is strong enough to visibly differ from MLE in early rounds without overwhelming real data in later rounds. In a real application, the prior would come from domain knowledge, previous experiments, or a deliberately weak default.

Intuition MAP behaves as if the gambler had already seen 3 phantom wins and 3 phantom losses on every arm before the game began. When Nk is small, those phantoms dominate — no arm can look like a guaranteed winner or a guaranteed dud. When Nk is large, the phantoms are washed out and MAP converges toward MLE.

Both algorithms see a two-way tie between arms 2 and 5, and both break it at random. They agree on the choice; they disagree on how dramatic the gap is. MLE is ready to declare arms 1, 3, 4 worthless after a single failure — MAP keeps them in contention with a score only 25% below the leaders.

VI. Takeaways

MLE is a pure empiricist — it trusts ratios of raw counts and nothing else. This makes it fragile under small samples, where a couple of lucky (or unlucky) pulls produce misleading 0.0 and 1.0 scores that the greedy rule then locks in on.

MAP softens that fragility by adding α₀ − 1 phantom successes and β₀ − 1 phantom failures to every count. For this script, that is 3 and 3 — enough to keep every arm's early score in the plausible-middle zone, but too few to matter once real data accumulates. MAP is not a different kind of algorithm than MLE; it is MLE computed on counts that have been nudged toward the prior.

Both remain point-estimate methods. Neither represents the fact that an arm pulled twice and an arm pulled two hundred times carry wildly different levels of certainty. That is the information Thompson Sampling exploits by drawing from the full Beta posterior instead of collapsing it to a single number — which is why the script's Figure 2 shows TS's cumulative regret growing as O(log T) while MLE's grows linearly, with MAP sitting somewhere between, closer to MLE than to TS.
