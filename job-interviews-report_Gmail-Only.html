1Executive summary

The archive looks like a twenty-year story of 350 interview processes. It is not. Once duplicates, unrelated mail and unanswered recruiter blasts are removed, it is the record of 176 real hiring conversations, concentrated in one intense campaign (2012–2014), a long period of selective looking while employed (2015–2019), and a thin, agency-dominated tail (2020–2026).

  • Eight offers, all inside eight months. BD Biosciences, Goodyear, Apple, Leads360, Polaris Wireless, Intel, Wipro and JDSU all fall between January 24 and August 29, 2013. No offer is recorded before or after that window.
  • Three offers became work — for two weeks, ten weeks and eleven years. BD Biosciences (from Feb 11, 2013, about two weeks), Apple via Red Oak (Mar 4 – May 15, 2013), and JDSU, renamed Viavi Solutions, from 2013 until 2024. Goodyear and Wipro were signed and then declined; Intel was declined outright.
  • 21 on-site visits, not 60. Each was verified against the email text. 6 of the 14 visits before August 2013 produced offers; none of the 7 visits after that did.
  • The career arc runs opposite to the earlier write-up. Data-science roles dominated 2012–2019; chemometrics, spectroscopy and sensing rose from 12% of genuine processes in 2007–2014 to 59% after 2020.
  • Recruiters took over the inbox. The share of threads coming through agencies climbed 37% → 50% → 59% across the three eras, while genuine processes fell from 74 to 34. In 2025 there was not a single genuine process — only pitches.
  • Silence is the normal ending. 140 of 176 genuine processes (80%) stop without any recorded decision. Explicit rejections: 23.

Why this edition differs. The previous version, produced by another assistant, had internally inconsistent totals (350 vs 352 processes; 7 vs “eleven” offers), counted parking-lot notices and job-alert newsletters as interviews, labelled 26 recruiter ads as “on-site” because their titles contained the word Onsite, and described the career chronology backwards. Section 2.3 lists every correction.

2How this edition was built

2.1Source and method

The only source is the earlier HTML archive, which was built from Gmail metadata and snippets. It contains 350 threads. For 91 of them it preserves up to eight dated email excerpts; for the other 259 it keeps only a subject line, a date and an email count. Every thread was parsed into a table, and each was then read and classified by hand. Where a claim — an on-site visit, an offer, a withdrawal — could be checked against the excerpt text, it was; where it could not, it is marked as probable or left as no recorded outcome. Nothing is inferred beyond what is written, and every inference that is made is labelled as one.

Two housekeeping fixes were applied throughout: HTML entities that the earlier build had double-escaped (so that apostrophes displayed as &#39;) were decoded, and Chang’s mobile number and date of birth, which appeared in several excerpts, were redacted.

6.2Anatomy of the 2013 decisions

Read as a sequence of decisions, the 2013 offer season has a clear internal logic, even though no single email states it.

Decision 1 — Bay Area over relocation (February). The Goodyear offer was the most secure: full-time, signed, a sign-on bonus, relocation under way. Within a week of signing, Chang chose instead a contract at Apple with a three-month conversion clause and no guarantee. The family consideration he cited to Goodyear is the stated reason; the revealed preference is to stay in California and bet on converting a contract at a top-tier firm.

Decision 2 — keep options open when the bet stalled (March–June). From his first weeks at Apple he kept interviewing — Guardian Analytics near his Sunnyvale desk in March, Leads360 in March–May, Intel and Polaris in May. When the Apple access ended on May 15, he had a Leads360 offer in hand, an Intel on-site behind him and a Polaris verbal offer days away. The search never stopped; that is why the gap left by Apple was covered immediately.

Decision 3 — say no to good offers that did not fit (June–July). Intel was declined after an attempt to negotiate the salary up to $130k; Leads360 was allowed to lapse despite the family’s affection for Southern California; Wipro was signed and then released when it collided with the Red Oak non-compete. Each refusal has a different surface reason, but the pattern is consistent: Chang did not take an offer simply because it was an offer.

Decision 4 — choose the specialty (August). On August 29 he told Twitter he had taken another offer: JDSU, as an NIR applications specialist. Of the eight offers, it was the only one in his own field — and the one he kept for eleven years. The campaign that began in spring 2012 ends there; apart from one phone screen already in motion, the next new genuine process does not appear until November 2013 (H2Scan).

A contract clause shaped the outcome. The Wipro withdrawal shows that the Apple contract’s legal terms outlived the job itself. A non-compete signed with one vendor blocked a later offer that apparently routed back to the same client — a detail that is invisible on a résumé and decisive in the inbox.

6.3What converted — and what didn’t

Three variables separate the processes that produced offers from those that did not.

  • Timing. Every offer came in one eight-month window (January–August 2013). In that window Chang was searching full-time in effect, answering within hours and travelling freely. Outside it, the same résumé produced invitations but no offers.
  • Interview format. The offers came from panels and presentations where domain modelling was the subject — Goodyear’s data-modelling panel, Intel Labs’ graph-analytics day, the Polaris Wireless architect who interviewed him. The rejections cluster where the format was a generic screen or a coding interview (Facebook 2017 and 2022, KLA-Tencor 2017, Agilent 2018, EY 2019).
  • Contract vs. full-time. Contracts converted fastest: BD Biosciences and Apple moved from first contact to offer in weeks, Apple without any on-site. Full-time roles took longer and required relocation more often (Goodyear to Ohio, Intel to Oregon, Leads360 to Southern California).

What did not matter much was the brand of the employer. Amazon (two phone rounds and an on-site hold in 2012), DeepMind (an initial interview in October 2012) and Twitter (two phone rounds in 2013) all appear, but the offers came from a mix of household names and small firms.

6.4The domain arc: back to chemometrics

The previous edition claimed that early emails were dominated by chemometrics and that the “data scientist” label took over later. The archive shows the reverse. In 2007–2014, only 12% of genuine processes were chemometrics, spectroscopy or sensing roles, and 45% were data science. In 2020–2026, chemometrics accounts for 59% and data science for 15%.

The explanation is market fit, visible in the rejection pattern. General data-science hiring standardised during the 2010s around coding tests in Python, Java or C++, and large-scale engineering. 6.7The candidate’s operating style

Across two decades, the same habits recur — and several of them plausibly helped.

  • He sends the evidence first. PowerPoint or PDF summaries of his work went out ahead of interviews at HRL, Amazon, Sensor Platforms, Beckman Coulter, Goodyear, Impermium and Alakai (“as promised, please see attached”). Interviewers arrived already knowing what he had done.
  • He confirms fast and protects his calendar. Most confirmations are a one-word “confirmed” within minutes, but he routinely moves interviews to lunch hours or after 4 pm, asks for two weeks’ lead time for travel, and pushes back on 6 am Pacific slots set by East-Coast schedulers.
  • He negotiates. The BD letter was revised to remove an end date; Intel was asked to match $130k; Block Engineering was asked to cover an extra hotel night; Oxford was told $100/hr was firm.
  • He consults his family and says so. “I will discuss this with my family” (Intel); “we both still very much like southern California” (Leads360); the Goodyear reversal cites his wife.
  • He writes with humour. KPMG’s “simplest interview schedule I ever have”; lunch during interviews “usually don’t taste too well anyway”; the laptop he carries everywhere “just like US president’s bowling ball bag”; a poppy-seed-pastry joke about drug screens to Guardian Analytics.

6.8Silence as the normal outcome

140 of 176 genuine processes (80%) end with no decision on record. Some were resolved by phone and never written down; others were abandoned by one side. Explicit rejections number 23, and they are almost always brief. Withdrawals by Chang himself — Twitter, Takeda, Bayer, Block Engineering, the attempted Impermium cancellation — are more numerous than the archive’s labels suggested, and they belong in the record as decisions, not failures.

The practical implication for reading any interview archive is that absence of a rejection is not a signal. The meaningful signals are the rare explicit events: an on-site, a presentation request, a rate confirmation, an offer letter.

7Take-home message

The inbox records 350 threads, but the career was decided in eight months of 2013 — by an NIR job at JDSU that the archive barely noticed.

Eight offers arrived between January and August 2013. Chang worked two weeks at BD and ten at Apple, signed Goodyear and Wipro and walked away from both, declined Intel outright, let Leads360 lapse — and then took the one offer in his own specialty, stayed eleven years at JDSU/Viavi, and remained in the Bay Area. After that, interviewing became something he did from a position of employment, selectively and increasingly in his own field — where the specialist employers kept inviting him in even as the generalist tech funnel, built around coding tests, kept him out. The large volume of later mail is mostly recruiters, not opportunity.

The weightiest decision in the archive is also its quietest record: thirteen emails, no offer letter. For a personal history, the lesson is to read an inbox as a ledger of decisions, not a scoreboard of rejections: the rare explicit events — an offer, a signed letter, a withdrawal — carry nearly all the information.
